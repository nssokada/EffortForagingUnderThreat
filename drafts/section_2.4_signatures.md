### 2.4 The fitness function reveals structured determinants of foraging success

The joint W(u) model not only fits choice and vigor better than simpler alternatives, but also reveals systematic structure in how individuals succeed and fail at the foraging problem. Rather than treating deviations from optimal behaviour as noise, the model organises errors, survival, and overall performance along two computational axes: avoidance sensitivity (omega) and activation intensity (kappa).

**Avoidance and activation jointly predict patch selection but dissociate in vigor.** Because both parameters enter the same fitness function, higher omega and higher kappa both reduce the expected value of the heavy (high-reward, high-effort) option — omega by inflating the subjective cost of capture, kappa by inflating the subjective cost of effort. Consistent with this, both parameters negatively predicted the probability of choosing the heavy cookie in both samples (omega: beta = -0.181, 95% HDI [-0.191, -0.171]; confirmatory: beta = -0.184, 95% HDI [-0.195, -0.174]; kappa: beta = -0.131, 95% HDI [-0.150, -0.114]; confirmatory: beta = -0.108, 95% HDI [-0.128, -0.088]). In the vigor channel, however, the parameters dissociated: higher kappa predicted lower pressing intensity across the task (Fig. 4c; beta = -0.195, 95% HDI [-0.217, -0.173]; confirmatory: beta = -0.196, 95% HDI [-0.217, -0.176]), whereas omega showed only a weak positive association with vigor (beta = +0.044, 95% HDI [+0.013, +0.074]; confirmatory: beta = +0.052, 95% HDI [+0.022, +0.081]). The two parameters therefore converge on what participants choose but diverge in how hard they work.

**Overcautious errors are structured by avoidance sensitivity.** When participants deviated from the model-predicted optimal policy, they predominantly erred in one direction: choosing the low-reward option when the high-reward option had higher expected value. Approximately 79% of all suboptimal choices were overcautious rather than reckless. The rate of these overcautious errors scaled monotonically with omega in both samples (Fig. 4b; beta = +0.171, 95% HDI [+0.155, +0.187]; confirmatory: beta = +0.123, 95% HDI [+0.109, +0.137]), indicating that suboptimal choices were organised by individual differences in capture-cost weighting rather than reflecting undirected variability.

**Avoidance sensitivity predicts survival; activation intensity does not.** Higher omega predicted increased escape probability on attack trials in both samples (Fig. 4a; beta = +0.060, 95% HDI [+0.033, +0.087]; confirmatory: beta = +0.047, 95% HDI [+0.020, +0.076]). Higher kappa did not (beta = +0.027, 95% HDI [-0.001, +0.054]; confirmatory: beta = +0.017, 95% HDI [-0.012, +0.044]), despite robustly predicting reduced motor output. This separation indicates that the choice channel — what participants chose to pursue — governed survival outcomes under threat, while the vigor channel — how hard they pressed — did not independently contribute to escape.

**Not all avoidance is adaptive: threat-driven avoidance outperforms effort-driven avoidance.** If omega is uniformly protective, higher omega should always improve foraging performance. Instead, what matters is the *balance* between the two cost weights. We indexed each participant's position in (omega, kappa) space as the angle between their standardised parameter estimates, with low angles corresponding to threat-driven avoidance and high angles to effort-driven avoidance. This angle reliably predicted the proportion of optimal choices in both samples (Fig. 4d; beta = -0.043, 95% HDI [-0.058, -0.028]; confirmatory: beta = -0.054, 95% HDI [-0.073, -0.037]). Participants whose avoidance tracked threat outperformed those whose avoidance tracked effort. The reason is structural: threat varies across trials (T in {0.1, 0.5, 0.9}), so threat-driven avoidance selectively reduces risk on dangerous trials while preserving reward opportunities on safe ones. Effort costs are fixed by cookie type, so effort-driven avoidance forgoes high-reward options indiscriminately regardless of danger.

**The joint configuration carries performance variance the marginal parameters do not.** Beyond the preregistered per-parameter signatures, we tested whether each participant's position on the model-implied fitness landscape predicted foraging outcomes after controlling for the marginal effects of omega and kappa (post-hoc). We computed the model-derived earnings landscape over a grid of (log(omega), log(kappa)) using population estimates of the remaining parameters and defined each participant's shortfall as the gap between their landscape-predicted earnings and the landscape maximum. After partialling out log(omega) and log(kappa), the shortfall replicated as a predictor of escape rate (beta = -0.319, 95% HDI excluding zero; confirmatory: beta = -0.363), total earnings (beta = -0.446; confirmatory: beta = -0.491), and proportion optimal (beta = -0.741; confirmatory: beta = -0.633), with all six effects excluding zero in both samples. The shortfall is a nonlinear function of (omega, kappa) that linear regression cannot capture; its substantial residual contribution shows that foraging success depends on the joint geometric position on the W(omega, kappa) landscape, not merely on the marginal cost weights.

**Cross-channel validation (post-hoc).** The model claims that omega and kappa enter both the choice and vigor channels through a shared fitness function. A particularly stringent test of this claim is whether the parameter fit predominantly from the choice channel (omega) makes correct predictions about the vigor channel — a channel the inference machinery never sees a press rate from when fitting omega. The partial effect of omega on mean anticipatory pressing rate, after partialling out kappa, was beta = +0.485 (95% HDI [+0.416, +0.545]) in exploratory and beta = +0.473 (95% HDI [+0.408, +0.535]) in confirmatory. The effect held in trial-level models with subject random intercepts controlling for cookie type and threat condition (beta approximately +0.12 in both samples). Because omega is fit only from binary choices, this cross-channel prediction is a structural commitment of the joint W(u) model that the data could have falsified.

**Avoidance sensitivity is threat-tuned, not uniform (post-hoc).** Trial-level multilevel logistic regressions with parameter-by-stimulus interactions revealed that the threat x omega interaction replicated in both samples with the predicted negative sign (beta = -0.075, 95% HDI [-0.133, -0.014] in exploratory; beta = -0.151, 95% HDI [-0.207, -0.094] in confirmatory). Higher omega is therefore not a blanket tendency to avoid but a specifically threat-tuned one: high-omega participants are more responsive to trial-level variation in T when selecting patches. None of the other three parameter-by-stimulus interactions (threat x kappa, distance x omega, distance x kappa) replicated.

Together, these results show that foraging behaviour under threat is not only governed by a unified computation, but structured in how it fails. Deviations from optimality reflect systematic differences in how competing costs are weighted; survival depends on the choice channel rather than the vigor channel; and adaptive performance depends on aligning avoidance with the variable structure of environmental danger rather than with fixed effort costs. The post-hoc cross-channel and threat-tuning tests further demonstrate that the joint W(u) model captures genuine computational structure — the parameters cross-predict between channels, operate through their predicted stimulus dimensions, and carry nonlinear joint variance the marginal effects do not absorb.
