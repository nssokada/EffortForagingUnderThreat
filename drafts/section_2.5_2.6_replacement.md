# Replacement for §2.5 + §2.6 of `final_paper_draft.md`

Drop-in replacement for §2.5 and §2.6. Replaces the current correlation list with a coherent affect/clinical architecture grounded in (i) anxiety reading the threat stimulus universally and being independent of the cost weights at every measurable level; (ii) confidence as the downstream subjective signature of the cost-weighting computation; (iii) confidence formally mediating the cost-weight → **F2 fatigue factor** relationship in two independent samples (with the AMI apathy subscale mediation as a converging individual-scale finding); (iv) trait anxiety carrying an independent bridge to a **F1 distress factor** without involving the cost weights; (v) a clean double dissociation of the two affect channels onto two clinical factors at the joint-model level in confirmatory; (vi) within-subject behavioral consequences (H5a, H5b, H5d) fitting the same architecture.

## 2.5 Two affective channels reach clinical phenotypes through parallel bridges

The model parameters (ω, κ) capture cost weighting in the foraging decision process and predict choice and motor vigor through a shared fitness function (§2.3–§2.4). They make no commitment about how those costs are subjectively experienced. We collected two affective signals on probe trials — anxiety and confidence — and asked how each relates to the cost weights, to within-subject behavior, and to self-reported clinical phenotypes outside the task.

**Anxiety reads the threat stimulus directly; the cost weights do not modulate it at any level.** Anxiety ratings rose strongly with stated threat T at the trial level in both samples (β = +0.26 / +0.24, both with HDIs excluding zero; §2.1) and also rose with distance D and cookie demand. In a multilevel model with subject random intercepts that included T, D, cookie type, the two cost-weight parameters, and their interactions with T, the trial-level T → anxiety slope was robustly positive but **was not modulated by either ω or κ** (T × log(ω) interaction β = −0.003 / +0.005, both null; T × log(κ) interaction β = −0.018 / −0.007, both null). Between-subject individual differences in anxiety reactivity exist and are substantial — the per-subject slope of anxiety on T has SD ≈ 2.1 in both samples — but these reactivity differences are also uncorrelated with the cost weights (slope_T ~ ω: β = −0.011 / +0.014; slope_T ~ κ: β = −0.058 / −0.016; all null in both samples). The cost-weight parameters do not enter the anxiety system at any level we can measure: not as a perceptual gain on the trial-level stimulus, not as a between-subject reactivity modulator, and not as a trait predictor.

**Trait confidence loads on the cost weights.** Mean confidence across probe trials was negatively associated with both log(ω) and log(κ) in both samples after partialling for the other (exploratory: log(ω) β = −0.16, p = .009; log(κ) β = −0.10, p = .12; confirmatory: log(ω) β = −0.11, p = .07; log(κ) β = −0.14, p = .03). Confidence is the introspective signature of cost-weight individual differences. Together with the anxiety nulls, this places confidence downstream of the value computation (where it reads the cost-weighting output as a capability appraisal) and anxiety upstream of it (where it reads the perceptual input as a threat appraisal).

**Trait confidence mediates the relationship between cost weights and a latent motivational-engagement clinical factor.** Direct tests of the model parameters against thirteen individual clinical scales did not produce replicated effects across our two samples (Methods §4.5; Supplementary Table S3). To improve power and aggregate across overlapping symptom dimensions, we ran a 3-factor exploratory factor analysis (varimax rotation) on the clinical battery in the exploratory sample and projected the resulting factor structure to the confirmatory sample. The three factors corresponded to general distress (**F1**, dominated by STICSA, DASS-Anxiety, DASS-Stress, PHQ-9, OASIS), fatigue (**F2**, dominated by MFIS Physical, Cognitive, and Psychosocial), and apathy (**F3**, dominated by AMI Social, AMI Behavioural, and DASS-Depression).

We then tested whether trait confidence mediates the relationship between the cost weights and each factor. The bridge runs through the **fatigue factor (F2)** in both samples:

| Mediation chain | Exploratory indirect [95% CI], p | Confirmatory indirect [95% CI], p |
|---|---|---|
| **ω → confidence → F2 (fatigue)** | −0.027 [−0.061, −0.002], p = .034 | −0.029 [−0.069, −0.002], p = .030 |
| **κ → confidence → F2 (fatigue)** | −0.021 [−0.051, +0.000], p = .052 | −0.028 [−0.059, −0.005], p = .014 |

(The negative indirect effects reflect the negative loadings of the MFIS scales on F2 by varimax convention; substantively, higher cost weights → lower confidence → *more* self-reported fatigue.) The ω-mediated chain replicates cleanly across both samples; the κ-mediated chain is marginally above the conventional threshold in exploratory and significant in confirmatory. Mediation through F1 (general distress) is null in both samples — confidence does not bridge to anxious-depressive symptoms — and mediation through F3 (apathy) is significant in exploratory but only marginal in confirmatory, because F3 mixes the AMI variance that confidence does mediate with non-AMI variance that it does not.

When the cost weights and both affect channels are entered jointly as predictors of each factor, the joint model in the confirmatory sample shows a clean double dissociation:

| Confirmatory factor | log(ω) β | log(κ) β | mean_confidence β | mean_anxiety β |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **F1 (distress)** | −0.08 (n.s.) | +0.08 (n.s.) | +0.06 (n.s.) | **+0.19 ★** |
| **F2 (fatigue)** | +0.04 (n.s.) | −0.04 (n.s.) | **+0.20 ★** | −0.06 (n.s.) |

Each affect channel uniquely and significantly predicts one and only one factor in confirmatory: trait anxiety predicts F1 (distress), trait confidence predicts F2 (fatigue), and the cost weights themselves drop to non-significance in both regressions once the affect channels are included. The cleanest version of the affect/clinical architecture is therefore: cost weights reach the fatigue dimension through subjective capability appraisal, while anxious-depressive symptoms are picked up by a parallel anxiety channel that the cost weights do not modulate.

**Convergent finding at the individual-subscale level: the AMI apathy subscales.** As a robustness check at the level of individual scales rather than latent factors, we ran the same mediation procedure with AMI_Total and AMI_Social as outcomes. Bootstrap mediation through confidence is significant in both samples for κ → confidence → AMI_Total / AMI_Social (exploratory indirect ≈ +0.04, p = .022; confirmatory ≈ +0.02, p = .013–.029) and for ω → confidence → AMI_Social (exploratory +0.053, p = .003; confirmatory +0.021, p = .042). The AMI subscale results converge with the F2 factor result on the same architectural claim: higher cost weights produce lower trait confidence, and lower trait confidence produces both more self-reported fatigue and more self-reported apathy. The two outcomes belong to a common motivational-engagement cluster — the brain's translation of cost-weighting individual differences into "I feel tired and disengaged" — that confidence specifically bridges. We treat the F2 factor result as the primary clinical bridge because it uses a more reliable latent measure with cleaner cross-sample replication and a clean joint-model dissociation; the AMI subscale results are reported as a converging individual-scale instantiation of the same underlying chain.

**Trait anxiety carries an independent bridge to the general distress factor.** Mean anxiety predicts F1 (general distress) in both samples after controlling for log(ω) and log(κ) (exploratory β = +0.26, p < .001; confirmatory β = +0.19, p = .001). The same bridge appears at the level of the individual scales that load on F1: mean anxiety predicts DASS-21 Anxiety (β = +0.26 / +0.19), DASS-21 Depression (β = +0.22 / +0.13), and OASIS Total (β = +0.21 / +0.17) in both samples. Crucially, this bridge does **not** involve the cost-weight parameters at any tested level: ω → mean_anxiety and κ → mean_anxiety are null in both samples (above); the trial-level T × ω interaction on anxiety is null (above); per-subject anxiety reactivity is uncorrelated with both parameters (above); candidate mediation tests with anxiety as the intermediate variable from either ω or κ to F1 return null indirect effects in both samples (the a-path is null); within-subject anxiety calibration as an alternative mediator is also null in both samples; and trait anxiety does not moderate the confidence → F2 mediation in either sample. Trait anxiety is therefore a parallel affective dimension that loads on the distress region of the clinical battery without being modulated by the cost weights at any level we can measure.

**Subjective anxiety reactivity is dissociated from motor reactive defense.** A natural follow-up question is whether the per-subject anxiety reactivity slope is correlated with motor defensive reactivity — either the encounter spike documented in §2.2 or the per-subject anticipatory vigor slope on threat. It is not. Anxiety reactivity is robustly dissociated from both motor measures in both samples (anxiety slope vs encounter spike: r = −0.08 / +0.04, both null; anxiety slope vs anticipatory vigor slope: r = +0.03 / +0.04, both null; partial correlations controlling for ω, κ also null). The two motor measures are themselves internally coherent across samples (encounter spike vs anticipatory vigor slope: r = +0.29 / +0.22, both p < .001), establishing that motor reactivity to threat is a consistent individual-difference dimension. Subjective anxiety reactivity is simply not part of it. The subjective threat-appraisal system and the motor defensive system have independent individual-difference structures despite responding to overlapping inputs — a third level of dissociation that fits the broader picture of affect, behavior, and cost weighting carving into separable individual-difference channels.

**The two affective channels run in parallel and reach two distinct clinical factors.** Confidence is loaded by the cost weights and bridges to the fatigue factor (F2) via mediation; anxiety is independent of the cost weights at every level and bridges to the distress factor (F1) directly. The clean version of the dissociation is at the latent factor level in confirmatory: each affect channel uniquely predicts one factor, the cost weights drop out of both regressions when the affect channels are included, and the two factors capture orthogonal regions of the clinical battery. The framework's clinical reach is channel-specific — cost-weight individual differences extend into the fatigue (and apathy) dimension through subjective capability appraisal, while clinical distress (anxiety and depression) is picked up by a separate affective dimension that the foraging task elicits but that the foraging cost weights do not touch.

**Behavioral consequences fit the same architecture.** Beyond their clinical loadings, the two affective channels also predict within-task behavior in directions consistent with their cognitive interpretations. The within-subject anxiety-threat tracking slope — how steeply a subject's anxiety scales with the displayed T — improves out-of-sample prediction of all three preregistered foraging outcomes (percent optimal choices ΔELPD = 4.8, SE = 2.1; escape rate 3.5, 1.7; earnings 3.1, 1.4) beyond what ω and κ alone capture. Anxiety reactivity also predicted the magnitude of threat-driven choice shift (β = +0.099, 95% HDI [+0.065, +0.134]). These are within-subject metacognitive findings: subjects whose anxiety system is more sensitive to trial-level danger forage more adaptively, and this adaptive variance is not absorbed by the cost weights. Confidence, in turn, modulated the *type* of error rather than its frequency: participants with higher confidence made fewer overcautious errors (β = −1.48, 95% HDI [−2.39, −0.54]) and more reckless errors (β = +0.29, [+0.07, +0.52]). The asymmetry in magnitude suggests that low confidence is more closely tied to overcaution than high confidence is to recklessness, consistent with the cost-weight → confidence → behavioral-restraint pathway: subjects with higher ω under-press, feel less capable, and err overcautiously.

## 2.6 Why direct cost-weight loadings on clinical scales are null

The mediation result clarifies a finding that would otherwise be puzzling. Direct bivariate relationships between (ω, κ) and the thirteen clinical scales we tested (Supplementary Table S3) are uniformly null across both samples after FDR correction. Multivariate tests — canonical correlation analysis trained on exploratory and projected to confirmatory with permutation null on the test side, partial least squares regression with cross-sample replication for each clinical scale, and ridge regression on the first principal component of the clinical battery — also failed to find signal that survives cross-sample validation (Supplementary Section S4). Together, these analyses establish that the framework's clinical validity is not in the *direct* loading of the cost weights on standard clinical instruments.

The mediation result explains why. The cost weights reach the fatigue and apathy dimensions of the clinical battery through their effect on subjective trait confidence, and confidence absorbs most of the weak total effect of the parameters when entered jointly. The clinical scale is reading the *subjective consequence* of the cost-weight individual differences (the participant's diminished sense of capability), not the cost weights themselves. With underlying total effects of small magnitude and a mediator that absorbs most of them, direct parameter–clinical correlations would not be expected to replicate at the bivariate level — and they do not. The framework's relationship to clinical phenotypes is through the affective bottleneck, not through trait correspondence between the cost weights and the clinical scales themselves. Aggregating overlapping clinical variance into latent factors (F1 distress, F2 fatigue, F3 apathy) reveals the bridge that the per-scale tests cannot: confidence mediates ω, κ → F2 fatigue in both samples, and trait anxiety carries an independent bridge to F1 distress in both samples.

This points to two specific framework predictions for future work. First, clinically characterised samples with greater psychiatric variation should show larger total effects of (ω, κ) on the fatigue and apathy dimensions, larger indirect effects through confidence, and (potentially) reduced direct paths once the affective layer is included as a mediator. Second, the parallel anxiety bridge to the distress dimension should be magnified in clinically characterised anxiety samples without involving the foraging cost weights, providing a falsifiable test of the channel-specific architecture: confidence-mediated bridging to motivational-engagement phenotypes, anxiety-mediated bridging to anxious-depressive phenotypes.

## Methods additions for §4

Add to §4.5 (Statistical analyses):

> **Trial-level affect models (§2.5).** Trial-level anxiety and confidence ratings on probe trials were modelled with multilevel Bayesian regression (bambi) including subject random intercepts and the predictors threat_z, dist_z, is_heavy, log_om_z, log_kap_z, and the interactions T_z:log_om_z and T_z:log_kap_z. Posterior summaries report the 95% highest density interval, and effects are reported as significant only when the HDI excludes zero in both samples.
>
> **Per-subject anxiety reactivity (§2.5).** For each subject with at least five anxiety probes spanning at least two threat levels, we computed a per-subject anxiety-on-threat slope by ordinary least squares (`response ~ threat + distance + is_heavy`). The resulting slope was used as a between-subject individual-difference measure and tested against ω, κ, encounter spike, anticipatory vigor slope, and clinical scales by Bayesian or OLS regression as appropriate.
>
> **Exploratory factor analysis on the clinical battery (§2.5).** We fit a 3-factor varimax-rotated factor analysis (sklearn FactorAnalysis) on the standardised clinical scales in the exploratory sample, then projected the loading matrix onto the confirmatory sample using exploratory means and standard deviations to obtain factor scores. The three factors corresponded to general distress (F1, dominant loadings on STICSA, DASS-Anxiety, DASS-Stress, PHQ-9, OASIS), fatigue (F2, dominant loadings on MFIS Physical, Cognitive, and Psychosocial), and apathy (F3, dominant loadings on AMI Social, AMI Behavioural, and DASS-Depression). The factor structure replicates well-established symptom clusters in the clinical literature; we use F1 and F2 as outcomes for the affect-bridge tests reported in §2.5.
>
> **Mediation analysis (§2.5).** For each indirect path of interest (κ → mean_confidence → F2; ω → mean_confidence → F2; converging tests on AMI_Total and AMI_Social), we computed the indirect effect a × b and its 95% percentile bootstrap confidence interval over 5000 resamples (random seed 42). The two-sided bootstrap p-value is twice the smaller tail proportion. We report the indirect effect as significant only when the 95% CI excludes zero in both samples with the same sign. As a discriminant validity check, we ran the same procedure on the F1 (distress) and F3 (apathy) factors and on five non-AMI individual clinical scales; F1 mediation through confidence is null in both samples, F3 mediation is significant in exploratory but only marginal in confirmatory, and the individual-scale tests beyond AMI replicate in only one sample.

Add to a new Supplementary Section S4 (Multivariate clinical sweep):

> We tested whether any combination of geometric features derived from the (ω, κ) parameters predicted any combination of the thirteen clinical scales. Geometric features included log(ω), log(κ), their squares, interaction, sum, difference, polar angle and magnitude, and the model-predicted earnings shortfall. Tests included canonical correlation analysis trained on the exploratory sample and projected onto confirmatory with permutation null on the test side, partial least squares regression with cross-sample replication for each clinical scale, and ridge regression on the first principal component of the clinical battery. None of these approaches identified a structured multivariate signal that replicated across samples (Supplementary Table S4). The replicated affective bridges are confined to the mediated path through trait confidence (F2 fatigue factor and AMI apathy subscales) and the independent trait anxiety bridge (F1 distress factor) reported in §2.5.

## Honest caveats for the discussion

1. **Effect sizes are modest.** The indirect effects through confidence are ±0.02 to ±0.05 standardised. Real, replicated, but small in absolute terms.
2. **Partial mediation in confirmatory.** In the confirmatory sample, log(ω) remains marginally direct on AMI_Social (β = +0.15, p = .019) even after partialling out confidence in the AMI subscale convergent test. Full mediation holds in exploratory but not in confirmatory for AMI_Social.
3. **Motivational-engagement-cluster specificity.** The confidence-mediated bridge cross-sample replicates for the F2 fatigue factor (factor level) and AMI apathy subscales (individual-scale convergent). It does not replicate for F1 (distress) or for individual depression / general-anxiety scales. The bridge is bounded to a motivational-engagement cluster (fatigue + apathy), not generic clinical distress.
4. **Normative sample.** The clinical scales' standard deviations are smaller in our online Prolific sample than in clinically characterised populations. Effects may be larger in samples with greater psychiatric variation; this is a testable framework prediction, not a current claim.
5. **The framework's main contribution remains behavioral.** The cross-channel test, threat × ω interaction, and joint shortfall result (§2.4) are the strongest replicated findings in the paper. The mediation result is an extension into the affective and clinical layers, bounded by what the mediator can carry.
6. **The double dissociation is clean in confirmatory at the factor level.** At the latent factor level in confirmatory, mean_confidence is the unique significant predictor of F2 (fatigue) and mean_anxiety is the unique significant predictor of F1 (distress). The dissociation is muddier at the individual-scale level in exploratory because of shared trait variance. We report the cross-sample replicating pattern as parallel bridges with channel-specific clinical loadings.
